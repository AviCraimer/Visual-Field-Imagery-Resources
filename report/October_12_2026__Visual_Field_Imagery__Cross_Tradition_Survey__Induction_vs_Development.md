# Developing visual field imagery: what the sources tell a practitioner to do

Ninety-three sources are now kept. Earlier records came mostly from keyword-selected digests; many have since been read in full in their original language, so confidence is high for roughly forty records and medium for most of the rest. Each claim below is labelled by kind: a classical text prescribing, a teacher asserting, or a practitioner reporting.

## 1. What is being claimed

Two terms are used throughout. **Visual field imagery (VFI)** is light or form that appears in the visual field, behind closed lids or before open eyes, whether or not it seems to be inside or outside the body. **Mind's eye imagery (MEI)** is imagery held in imagination without occupying the visual field. Both terms are neutral on two further questions: whether the imagery is willed or spontaneous, and whether it is fantasy or true perception. Most of the sources here concern light, so most of the VFI discussed is luminous; 'light' below means visual field light unless stated otherwise.

The question for this section is narrow: does a source place what it describes in the visual field (VFI) or in the mind's eye (MEI)? Many sources also distinguish true imaginal vision from fantasy, a distinction about the status of what is seen, not about where it is seen. Similarly, when a source calls an appearance 'mental' or 'only in the mind', it is often making a claim about what the appearance is made of (its ontology), not about where or how it is perceived (its phenomenology). Both of these are set aside below; only statements about how and where the appearance is experienced count. A third axis also needs keeping apart: whether an image is willed or comes unbidden. Willed images are usually easier to produce in the mind's eye, but the two axes are independent. Many mind's-eye images arrive unbidden, and some sources report willing light in the visual field: Newton found that after a few sun exposures he could recall the after-image in the dark by attention alone, more easily each time [mod-newton-letter-sun-phantasm], and Pa-Auk's white sign, seen wherever one looks, can be reshaped at will [bud-pa-auk-knowing-and-seeing]. So whether something is willed is not a test of where it is seen. With that caution, the sources that bear on the location question do so in five ways.

**Instructions not to visualise.** Some forbid building an image, so that whatever appears cannot be mistaken for the practitioner's own picture. This removes one source of confusion; it does not by itself show that what then appears is in the visual field. Kirpal Singh tells disciples not to picture a face or a candle, only to look [snt-kirpal-light-of-kirpal]. Sagan rules out visualisation and counts a faint whitish haze in the dark field as the real beginning [mod-sagan-awakening-third-eye]. Mueller, observing himself, found that the images he tried to call up stayed unlit and still, while luminous figures appeared in the dark field suddenly and unprompted [mod-mueller-phantastische-gesichtserscheinungen]; a correspondent printed by Galton contrasts a dim imagined image with a daylight-clear one seen in the field. For these two observers the luminous images happened also to be the involuntary ones; Newton's experience shows that need not hold [mod-galton-inquiries-human-faculty, mod-newton-letter-sun-phantasm].

**Open and closed eyes.** Some sources test or locate the light by what the eyelids do. Ibn 'Arabi gives a test for something seen with open eyes, such as the form of a person: close the eyes. If it is still there, it is in the imagination; if it disappears, it was perceived out in the space where it appeared, in front of the eyes. He states the test for forms, not for light [suf-ibn-arabi-risalat-al-anwar]. Kubra's point of light appears first in trance, then with the lids closed, then as stars by daylight with open eyes [suf-kubra-fawaih-al-jamal]. Aurobindo says what is seen with closed eyes can later be seen with them open [hin-aurobindo-letters-on-yoga-3]. Longchenpa says the Togal lights are seen through the eyes, with open eyes, against sky or darkness [vaj-geisshuesler-sky-gazing].

**Light in or before the eye.** A third group ties the light to the eye itself: the Zohar to the shut eye pressed or rolled [jew-zohar-closed-eye-colours], the Zhen'gao to pressing the eye corners [dao-zhengao-j9-10], the Vijnanabhairava to closing the eyes with the hands until a point is seen [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra], Russell to a glow that replaces the gloom behind the lids [wes-ae-candle-of-vision]. Goethe treats the after-image as fixed on one place of the retina, and the light raised by pressure as light without outer light [mod-goethe-farbenlehre-physiologische-farben]. Bates's patients insisted they saw their palming lights with their eyes [mod-bates-perfect-sight].

**A third kind of seeing.** Teresa supplies the most careful phenomenology of a seeing that fits neither bin. She says she never saw anything with the bodily eyes, only with the eyes of the soul (*ojos del alma*), yet calls that sight clearer than bodily sight [chr-teresa-life, chr-teresa-interior-castle]. She is precise about how it differs from what imagination can do. Imagination can build Christ's figure and its whiteness piece by piece and hold it; the vision arrives whole in an instant, shows what was never thought of, lasts about as long as a flash of lightning, cannot be altered, kept or summoned, and vanishes if one tries to study a detail. If you can gaze at it at length, she says, you made it yourself [chr-teresa-interior-castle]. Its light is a soft whiteness and infused brilliance that does not dazzle and makes sunlight look tarnished, a light with no night, like a sun seen through a diamond; open or closed eyes make no difference [chr-teresa-life]. Her case shows that marks often taken as signs of seeing in the visual field (involuntary, instantaneous, unalterable) can belong to an interior sight as well, Where she places it is less settled than her disclaimer suggests. She denies the bodily eyes, yet calls the eyes of the soul clearer than bodily sight and describes the vision in fully visual terms (brightness, whiteness, sudden appearance, a face seen), and the eyelids make no difference to it. That is compatible with a vision that occupies the visual field while not coming through the eyes; she does not settle the question either way. She gives no method; she warns against seeking it.

**Models of crossing.** Several sources start from deliberate MEI and expect it to become VFI. Pa-Auk has the yogi look at a kasina, close the eyes and visualise it until the sign is seen as clearly as with eyes open [bud-pa-auk-knowing-and-seeing]; the Visuddhimagga's counterpart sign stands free of the disk 'in space' [bud-visuddhimagga-nanamoli]; the Dhammakaya manual begins with an imagined sphere and insists the aim is to see it [bud-dhammakaya-luang-phor-sodh-manual]; the Zhen'gao and the Bodhicitta treatise do likewise [dao-zhengao-j9-10, bud-bodhicitta-sastra-t1665]. Tibetan deity practice shows both outcomes. In the Illusory Body practice translated by Evans-Wentz, one gazes at a painted deity reflected in a mirror until it seems animated, then holds it in the space between mirror and self until it seems solid enough to touch, eyes open; looking life-like is called the standard test of a visualization [vaj-evans-wentz-tibetan-yoga-1935]. A Tara sadhana promises that the meditator will see her with his own eyes, without describing the seeing [vaj-bhattacharyya-iconography-1924]. The Chakrasambhara Tantra, by contrast, asks only that the deity be vivid to the mind's eye. Vital gives the most explicit theory of how such an image passes from the mind's eye into the senses. In the practice of divestment the practitioner stills the imaginative faculty, then uses it to picture his ascent through the roots of his soul until the images of the lights are engraved in thought "as though he were picturing and seeing them". Light drawn down from there takes a material image in the five inner senses of the imaginative faculty, so that he understands it as though seeing it with his actual eyes; in some cases the images are transferred outward to the five outer senses, and then he sees and hears with his physical senses. In lesser prophets the outer senses recoil and only the inner senses receive it [jew-vital-shaarei-kedushah]. This is a two-stage account: deliberate imaginal work first, then an involuntary descent that may or may not reach the visual field.

**Where it stays unclear.** Several important sources do not settle the question. Pa-Auk's white sign is seen wherever one looks, yet can be reshaped at will [bud-pa-auk-knowing-and-seeing]. Vital says the first phase is imagination and the light afterwards presents itself as though seen [jew-vital-shaarei-kedushah]. Kalicharana, commenting on the Shat-chakra-nirupana, insists the sparks shine distinctly but calls them objects of mental vision [hin-avalon-serpent-power]. Most of the Vijnanabhairava's methods say only "contemplate" [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]. Tulsi Sahib uses verbs of seeing throughout but places the sight in an inner eye within the body [snt-tulsi-sahib-ghat-ramayan].

## 2. The technique families

**Looking into the closed-eye darkness.** Three Sant Mat teachers give the same instruction: a posture you can hold, eyes closed, body and breath forgotten, a gaze without strain into the middle of the darkness between and behind the eyebrows, with slow repetition of the given names [snt-kirpal-spiritual-elixir, snt-kirpal-teachings-meditation, snt-sawan-singh-spiritual-gems]. Sawan Singh sets an hour and a half daily [snt-sawan-singh-spiritual-gems]. Reported in order: sparks, steady light, a starry sky, a large star, sun and moon [snt-sawan-singh-spiritual-gems, snt-kirpal-light-of-kirpal]. Tulsi Sahib, the older classical source, gives a longer ladder: attention fixed on the sesame-seed point behind the eyes without a blink's break, night and day; a single star, lightning, five colours, a lamp seen through a temple door, a full moon, then a midday sun [snt-tulsi-sahib-ghat-ramayan]. The Vijnanabhairava prescribes long contemplation of darkness on a moonless night, or, with eyes closed, of the black expanse before one [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]. Mueller sits in a dark place, calm, unfed, eye muscles slack, thought refused; light-spots and mists come, then a dull inner daylight, then luminous images [mod-mueller-phantastische-gesichtserscheinungen]. Galton watched perfect darkness with the strain used to make out a sign-post at night [mod-galton-inquiries-human-faculty]. They conflict on strain, which Sant Mat and Mueller reject and Galton uses, and on initiation, which Sant Mat requires.

**Gazing at a source and keeping what remains.** Stein and Ingram give the fullest procedure: a candle three to five feet away, a minute or two of staring, eyes closed on the after-image until it is gone, repeated many times [bud-fire-kasina-book-stein-ingram]. Sivananda lists ten trataka objects and holds the image with closed eyes [hin-sivananda-kundalini-yoga]. Niranjanananda's Bihar School manual is the fullest yogic version: three minutes on a candle, then the after-image held at the eyebrow centre as a tiny luminous dot whose colour depends on how long one gazed, gathered again with the eyes still shut each time it fades, until it merges into the dark. The inner phase is lengthened until a round takes half an hour, and eleven rounds 'make one dharana', when the candle is seen in every detail with the eyes closed. The same procedure is applied to a black dot, a flower (first seen as a disc of the opposite colour), a deity's face framed in black, and the Sri Yantra, built up triangle by triangle over weeks. Combined outer and inner gazing is expressly for those who cannot yet hold an inner image [hin-niranjanananda-dharana-darshan]. Goethe gives the controlled form: five seconds on a sunlit white disc in a dark room, then the darkest corner or closed eyes; a bright yellowish centre with a purple rim, purple spreading inward, then blue, then colourless, shrinking away, revivable by blinking for about seven minutes [mod-goethe-farbenlehre-physiologische-farben]. Chia uses a low sun, candle or bulb and draws the after-image inward, but aims at felt energy, not persistent light [dao-chia-awaken-healing-light]. The Contemplation Sutra holds the setting sun until it stays with eyes shut or open [bud-contemplation-sutra-takakusu]. The Vijnanabhairava fixes the gaze on space dappled by sun or lamp [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra].

**Taking a sign from a prepared object.** In Theravada usage the sign (*nimitta*) is the object as it appears to the meditator, in three grades. The preparatory sign is the physical object itself, a coloured disc or the breath. The learning (or acquired) sign is that object reproduced inwardly: the Visuddhimagga says it has arisen when the disc appears with the eyes shut exactly as it does with them open. The counterpart sign is a purified version that stands free of the disc, bright and stable, which the Visuddhimagga says has neither colour nor shape, since if it had it would be cognizable by the eye [bud-visuddhimagga-nanamoli]. The texts call the signs mental, but that is an ontological classification: in Abhidhamma terms everything after the eye's contact with the disc is cognition at the mind door, so the Visuddhimagga's 'neither colour nor shape' and the Vimuttimagga's 'exists only in thought' [bud-vimuttimagga-ehara] say what the sign is, not how it appears. Their phenomenological descriptions point the other way: the learning sign appears with eyes shut exactly as with eyes open, the counterpart sign stands 'in space' [bud-visuddhimagga-nanamoli], and Buddhadasa, who calls both signs mental images, says the counterpart sign may hang in front of the meditator and can be moved at will [bud-buddhadasa-anapanasati]. Pa-Auk tells the yogi to visualize the kasina until it is as clear as with eyes open [bud-pa-auk-knowing-and-seeing]. The modern fire-kasina practitioners speak of the visual field directly, while often also calling it mental, which on this reading is no contradiction [bud-fire-kasina-book-stein-ingram]. The method itself: the Visuddhimagga prescribes a circle of sun or moonlight through a hole, or a lamp in a pot facing a wall, viewed with eyes moderately open [bud-visuddhimagga-nanamoli]; the Vimuttimagga uses water throwing sunlight on a wall [bud-vimuttimagga-ehara]; Pa-Auk holds the white sign for one to three hours [bud-pa-auk-knowing-and-seeing]. The Thai forest teachers do not use the three-sign scheme and advise not following such appearances [bud-maha-boowa-acariya-mun-biography, bud-thanissaro-with-each-and-every-breath].

**Concentration on something that is not light.** Pa-Auk keeps attention at the breath's touch point, where grey smoke, then white, then a morning-star brightness come [bud-pa-auk-knowing-and-seeing]. The suttas say to apply the mind to the perception of light, by night as by day, without procedure [bud-suttas-perception-of-light]. The Svetasvatara gives the oldest list: erect posture, senses drawn into the heart, breath restrained, a quiet sheltered place by water, and then fog, smoke, sun, fire, wind, fireflies, lightning, crystal and moon as forerunners of Brahman [hin-hume-thirteen-upanishads].

**Fixed gaze and posture.** Classical texts prescribe half-closed eyes on the nose tip [hin-hatha-yoga-pradipika-sinh]; open eyes on the nose tip or into space [hin-thirty-minor-upanishads-aiyar]; lids lowered until the nose tip is just visible, attention level between the brows, where the Golden Flower says the light enters of itself [dao-jinhua-zongzhi-chinese]; Togal's gazes on a cloudless sky or in a dark room [vaj-baker-tibetan-yoga-togal, vaj-geisshuesler-sky-gazing]; and an unwavering gaze on a clear sky, body still [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]. Sagan and Mehi say not to turn the eyeballs toward the brow [mod-sagan-awakening-third-eye, snt-mehi-philosophy-of-liberation].

**Repetition in seclusion.** Ghazali prescribes a solitary corner and unbroken repetition of the divine name [suf-ghazali-ihya-dhikr-seclusion-method]. Razi prescribes forty days in a small dark cell, eyes closed, with the shaykh's formula [suf-razi-mirsad-al-ibad]; Ibn 'Ata' Allah, a cell admitting no daylight and a sitting with head between the knees [suf-ibn-ata-allah-miftah-al-falah]; Kubra, Junayd's eight conditions of fasting, silence, seclusion, dhikr and the bond to the shaykh [suf-kubra-fawaih-al-jamal]. The Hesychasts also repeat a name with the breath drawn into the heart, but toward a different end, treated under Dissent [chr-xanthopoulos-century]. Sivananda's Japa Yoga, read only in its introduction, links japa to a deity image, not to seen light [hin-sivananda-japa-yoga].

**Building an image until it is seen.** The Dhammakaya manual moves an imagined crystal sphere through seven bases until a bright sphere arises [bud-dhammakaya-luang-phor-sodh-manual]. The Zhen'gao makes the half-closed eyes see five-coloured vapours [dao-zhengao-j9-10]. The Bodhicitta treatise contemplates a white moon disc in the heart until one sees one's own mind so shaped, a first glimpse counting as truth and constant seeing as the first ground; whether this seeing is visual is not said [bud-bodhicitta-sastra-t1665]. Kirpal Singh forbids visualising [snt-kirpal-light-of-kirpal], and Mueller found willed images stay unlit [mod-mueller-phantastische-gesichtserscheinungen]. The Tibetan Illusory Body practice uses a mirror: gaze at a painted deity reflected in it until the figure seems animated, then hold it, eyes open, in the space between mirror and self until it seems solid enough to touch [vaj-evans-wentz-tibetan-yoga-1935].

**Scrying.** Melville and Sepharial agree: a shaded room, the crystal screened in black, ten to fifteen minutes daily at one hour; the crystal dulls, shows star-like points, clouds, then clears [wes-melville-crystal-gazing, wes-sepharial-how-to-read-the-crystal]. Niranjanananda's crystal goes blank before visions come with the eyes open; gazing at one's shadow and then up at the sky leaves a silver haze in its shape [hin-niranjanananda-dharana-darshan]. Sagan's eye contact uses a partner or a mirror by candlelight against a blank wall: motionless, blinking as little as possible, attending to the fact of seeing rather than the image. The image blurs, halos and colours appear, faces change, and the room seems to darken into a glowing semi-darkness that he identifies with the purple light seen behind closed eyes. Each time sharp vision snaps back, the practitioner relaxes and lets it go again; palming at the end of a round brings inner light [mod-sagan-awakening-third-eye].

**Sense closure and eye pressure.** The Shat-chakra-nirupana's yoni mudra closes ears, eyes, nostrils and mouth with the fingers, holds the breath and meditates on Om at the brow; by repeated practice sparks shine above the triangle there, then a steady light like the morning sun [hin-avalon-serpent-power]. The Vijnanabhairava closes the eyes with the hands, presses or attends at the brows, and a point is seen that dissolves by degrees [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]. Rama Prasad gives the same finger closure in one verse [hin-rama-prasad-natures-finer-forces]. The Zohar's pressed eye is a demonstration, not a regimen [jew-zohar-closed-eye-colours]; the Zhen'gao presses the eye corners eighteen times until light is seen [dao-zhengao-j9-10]; Longchenpa has the beginner press the eyeballs lightly [vaj-geisshuesler-sky-gazing]. Bates's patients, palming without pressure, saw lights at once [mod-bates-perfect-sight]. Czaplicka reports a Samoyed novice, blindfolded and struck on the head, dazzled as with too much light before seeing spirits; it is a historical report, not a practice [shm-czaplicka-aboriginal-siberia].

**Dark room.** Randolph says sparks appear within minutes in a fully dark room [wes-randolph-seership]; Togal names a dark chamber and a forty-nine-day retreat [vaj-geisshuesler-sky-gazing].

**Breath and energy work.** The Wu-Liu manuals and the Golden Flower treat light as a timing sign: a flash between the brows during the firing of essence means continue, later flashes mean stop [dao-dacheng-jieyao, dao-wu-shouyang-xianfo-hezong, dao-huiming-jing-chinese]. Chia counts light in the head as one optional sign of circulating energy, equal to heat or tingling [dao-chia-awaken-healing-light].

**Waiting on gift.** Hilton's light springs little by little in restful darkness [chr-hilton-scale-of-perfection]. Aua's came in solitude after teaching failed [shm-rasmussen-iglulik-intellectual-culture]. Hildegard's was present from infancy [chr-hildegard-letter-guibert]; Motovilov's came through Seraphim's prayer and touch [chr-seraphim-motovilov-conversation]. Kabir says the guru removes the veil and the eyes open suddenly [snt-tagore-songs-of-kabir].

## 3. Breaking through versus developing

**What is offered to someone who sees nothing.** The answers are of six kinds.

*Supply the first light from outside.* A flame after-image gives a seen object at the first sitting. Stein reports a stable red dot within hours and brilliant colour by day nine of twenty-five [bud-fire-kasina-book-stein-ingram]. Goethe's sequence accounts for that dot: the bright disc's purple, blue and colourless phases are what any rested eye produces after a few seconds on a bright source [mod-goethe-farbenlehre-physiologische-farben]. Sivananda, as a teacher, gives trataka the same role, with onset from days to nine months, and adds that some never see lights and still progress [hin-sivananda-kundalini-yoga]. This remains the answer with the most dated practitioner testimony.

*Keep looking into the dark.* Sawan Singh tells a disciple who sees nothing to keep peering, and the darkness will turn to light [snt-sawan-singh-spiritual-gems]. Tulsi Sahib asks for attention unbroken by a single blink, night and day [snt-tulsi-sahib-ghat-ramayan]. Russell reports weeks or months of stupor first [wes-ae-candle-of-vision]. Galton, the one self-declared non-seer, believed his dark field uniform black, yet after habituating himself to strained watching in perfect darkness found faint, shifting patterns, which he says many will find [mod-galton-inquiries-human-faculty]. Mueller holds that everyone has traces and that those who lack them by day can find them before sleep [mod-mueller-phantastische-gesichtserscheinungen].

*Recognise what is already there.* Sagan asserts that most who think they see nothing overlook a faint haze [mod-sagan-awakening-third-eye]. Kubra says, in classical form, that one who closes the eyes and sees nothing is seeing the darkness of his own existence, the first stage [suf-kubra-fawaih-al-jamal]. Chia, as a teacher, says some people are not visually inclined and may never see colour, and that feeling does the same work [dao-chia-awaken-healing-light].

*Thin the self by regime.* Kubra's remedy is striving: less food, surrender to a shaykh, the eight conditions [suf-kubra-fawaih-al-jamal]. Ibn 'Ata' Allah says to stay on the formula until its fruit appears, waking or in dream [suf-ibn-ata-allah-miftah-al-falah]. The Zhen'gao concedes that at first there is not even a semblance and counts results in years [dao-zhengao-j9-10]. Avalon's text adds that the method is learnt by serving a guru, not from books [hin-avalon-serpent-power].

*Repeat harder.* The Dhammakaya manual says to keep imagining and reciting a hundred or a thousand times [bud-dhammakaya-luang-phor-sodh-manual]; the crystal manuals prescribe the same short sitting for months [wes-melville-crystal-gazing, wes-sepharial-how-to-read-the-crystal]. The Vijnanabhairava, by contrast, asks only steadiness and claims any one method suffices [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra].

*Borrow someone else's attention.* Tawakkul Beg saw little until his master intervened in person [suf-tawakkul-beg-initiation-by-mulla-shah]. Motovilov saw only when Seraphim prayed and laid hands on him [chr-seraphim-motovilov-conversation].

**Negative testimony.** One ex-member of the Divine Light Mission, promised a light brighter than a thousand suns, saw none at initiation, and non-seeing was blamed on his impurity; his account is silent on any light in later years and never describes the techniques [snt-expremie-journey-ajw].

**Sources that only develop.** The Wu-Liu manuals refuse induction: light comes after the work and light without the bodily sign is delusion [dao-dacheng-jieyao, dao-wu-shouyang-xianfo-hezong, dao-huiming-jing-chinese]. The Golden Flower says not to care whether there is light [dao-jinhua-zongzhi-chinese]. The Upakkilesa Sutta troubleshoots light already present [bud-mn128-upakkilesa-sutta]. The Bodhicitta treatise starts from a deliberately built disc and offers nothing to one who cannot build it [bud-bodhicitta-sastra-t1665].

**Sources that say it cannot be forced.** Teresa says no diligence obtains it [chr-teresa-life]. Al-Sanusi, via Rinn, says most affiliates never see the lights [suf-rinn-marabouts-et-khouan-1884]. Mueller could almost never summon a chosen luminous image [mod-mueller-phantastische-gesichtserscheinungen]. The Visuddhimagga puts success at one in a hundred or a thousand [bud-visuddhimagga-nanamoli]. Hildegard ascribes hers wholly to God [chr-hildegard-letter-guibert]; the Corpus Hermeticum says the way is not taught [wes-mead-corpus-hermeticum]. Galton calls the tendency hereditary and unequal [mod-galton-inquiries-human-faculty].

## 4. Physical induction and stimulus independence

**The baseline.** Goethe shows what the eye does with no practice at all: the bright-source after-image changes colour on a fixed timetable, fades within minutes, revives with blinking for about seven minutes, and always decays; he treats much longer persistence as weakness of the organ [mod-goethe-farbenlehre-physiologische-farben]. So the coloured dot of fire kasina and trataka, at that stage, is still stimulus-bound. Any claim of independence has to be a claim about what comes after it.

**Claims that the light outlasts the stimulus.** Newton, after brief sun exposures, found attention alone recalled the image in the dark, more easily each time [mod-newton-letter-sun-phantasm]. Stein and Ingram describe colour organising out of the murk after the dot has gone, though an LED refresh is still used [bud-fire-kasina-book-stein-ingram]. The Contemplation Sutra and Visuddhimagga require the image to stand free of its source [bud-contemplation-sutra-takakusu, bud-visuddhimagga-nanamoli]. Niranjanananda says the flower's counter-image grows more vivid and more like the flower with practice, but each time it vanishes he has the practitioner recollect it, so his later rounds hand the after-image over to recall, as Pa-Auk's do [hin-niranjanananda-dharana-darshan]. Longchenpa says light pressure is needed only for the first three or four days, after which appearances arise without it [vaj-geisshuesler-sky-gazing]; that record is pass_flagged on provenance and keeps the sky as visual ground, so it shows a tradition making the claim, not anyone achieving it. The Shat-chakra-nirupana's sparks under yoni mudra are followed by a steady light, but the text does not say whether it survives release of the fingers [hin-avalon-serpent-power]. Czaplicka's novice went on from the dazzle to visions, which may suggest the same [shm-czaplicka-aboriginal-siberia].

**Reports that it does not.** Strasburg pressed his eyes daily for two years with intense light; when the instruction changed to steadying without pressure, almost all of it went [snt-expremie-journey-strasburg]. Aurobindo says pressure cannot force colour [hin-aurobindo-letters-on-yoga-3]. The Zohar says its colours cannot be held [jew-zohar-closed-eye-colours]. Chia's after-image is explicitly stimulus-bound and he claims no more [dao-chia-awaken-healing-light]. Scrying light stays in the crystal [wes-melville-crystal-gazing].

**The inverse case.** Bates wanted a perfect black. His patients, eyes merely covered, saw lights at once; fatigue, hunger, worry and effort to suppress them made them stronger [mod-bates-perfect-sight]. Read in reverse, darkness yields light readily and strain feeds it, which sides with Galton against Mueller and Sant Mat on strain.

## 5. Once light appears

Advice divides into holding still and going through. Pa-Auk says stay at the touch point until the sign is stable, then rest on it for hours [bud-pa-auk-knowing-and-seeing]. The Upakkilesa Sutta lists states that make light vanish, among them doubt, dullness, elation and energy too tight or slack [bud-mn128-upakkilesa-sutta]. Mueller says eye movement scatters the images [mod-mueller-phantastische-gesichtserscheinungen]; Galton, that any other thought abolishes them [mod-galton-inquiries-human-faculty]. Longchenpa says to keep eyes and mind unmoving until the chains steady [vaj-geisshuesler-sky-gazing]. Kubra reads colours as a gauge of the heart [suf-kubra-fawaih-al-jamal]. The Sant Mat teachers say to look into the middle of the light until it bursts [snt-kirpal-spiritual-elixir, snt-sawan-singh-spiritual-gems], and Tulsi Sahib treats each light as a station to pass, the lamp bursting into a moon and the moon giving way to a sun [snt-tulsi-sahib-ghat-ramayan]. The Vijnanabhairava says to let the seen point dissolve and rest in what lies within it [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]. The Bodhicitta treatise enlarges the disc until it fills space [bud-bodhicitta-sastra-t1665]. Chia condenses any light into a dot and stores it at the navel, never leaving energy in the head [dao-chia-awaken-healing-light]. Ibn 'Arabi says not to stop at any unveiling [suf-ibn-arabi-risalat-al-anwar]. Several ascents end in a 'formless light' (Razi's light without colour, form or place, Diadochos's formless light of the mind) [suf-razi-mirsad-al-ibad, chr-diadochos-hundred-chapters]. That phrase is best read as a paradoxical marker of an experience beyond phenomena, the top of an ascent from form to formless, not as a further thing seen; the seen lights are the stages before it. Vital's account runs the other way, a descent from supernal influx to manifest image, so it is consistent that in his case form is added rather than shed [jew-vital-shaarei-kedushah].

## 6. Dissent and cautions

Several traditions say to leave the light alone. The Hesychast texts are the most sustained refusal. Gregory of Sinai tells the practitioner not to accept any light, fire or figure, outside or inside, and says seeking such things early makes a fantasist, not a hesychast; the beginner should expect warmth, not light [chr-gregory-sinai-philokalia]. Diadochos accepts only the formless self-light of a purified mind and says any shaped light or fiery form must be refused outright; one must not take up asceticism hoping to see [chr-diadochos-hundred-chapters]. Evagrius forbids wanting to see angels or Christ sensibly and holds that a demon can alter the light around the mind [chr-evagrius-on-prayer]. Xanthopoulos says light may come unsought after long prayer of the Name, but one should neither accept nor curse it, and without an illumined teacher should decline it; false light is fiery and smoky [chr-xanthopoulos-century]. Symeon warns that no one should claim light before mourning and tears [chr-symeon-chapters-philokalia]. These are classical texts prescribing; they do not deny the light, they deny its value as a target.

Others agree from other sides. Mahasi calls light a corruption of insight [bud-mahasi-progress-of-insight]. The Cloud author calls strained-for lights a deceit, and John of the Cross says to take no pleasure in visions [chr-cloud-of-unknowing, chr-john-of-the-cross-ascent]. Faqir Chand treats forms in the light as projection [snt-faqir-chand-unknowing-sage]. The Golden Flower says light seized as light becomes consciousness-light [dao-jinhua-zongzhi-chinese]. Razi treats every form and colour as a veil [suf-razi-mirsad-al-ibad]. Sivananda's Japa Yoga says to ignore coloured lights [hin-sivananda-japa-yoga]. Kabir rejects shutting eyes and ears altogether [snt-tagore-songs-of-kabir]. Abulafia's recitation names no seen light [jew-abulafia-or-ha-sekhel].

Safety, plainly. Sun: Newton lost the use of his eyes for days [mod-newton-letter-sun-phantasm]; Goethe reports sun images lasting days [mod-goethe-farbenlehre-physiologische-farben]; Bates and Chia recommend forms of sun-gazing, which should not be followed [mod-bates-perfect-sight, dao-chia-awaken-healing-light]. Eyes: Mehi warns that pressing causes pain and abnormality [snt-mehi-philosophy-of-liberation]; Goethe lists unbearable light from strong pressure among pathological effects [mod-goethe-farbenlehre-physiologische-farben]. Head: the Samoyed blows are dangerous and not a method [shm-czaplicka-aboriginal-siberia]. Breath: Teresa says holding it harms prayer [chr-teresa-interior-castle]; yoni mudra and the Svetasvatara both use retention [hin-avalon-serpent-power, hin-hume-thirteen-upanishads]. Energy: Chia warns that long brow focus can bring headaches, flashing lights and psychosis [dao-chia-awaken-healing-light]. Darkness, fasting and vigil: Ibn 'Ata' Allah warns of raving [suf-ibn-ata-allah-miftah-al-falah]; Kubra's shaykh feared madness or death [suf-kubra-fawaih-al-jamal]; Gregory says seeking early can cost sanity [chr-gregory-sinai-philokalia]; Stein and Ingram advise a guide through the fear stage [bud-fire-kasina-book-stein-ingram].

## 7. Where to start

**Candle after-image.** Sit three to five feet from a flame; look for a minute or two; close the eyes and follow the after-image until nothing organised remains; repeat [bud-fire-kasina-book-stein-ingram]. Begin short [hin-sivananda-kundalini-yoga]; Niranjanananda's eleven rounds give a schedule, three minutes outward and a lengthening inward phase [hin-niranjanananda-dharana-darshan]. Expect Goethe's colour sequence and do not mistake it for attainment [mod-goethe-farbenlehre-physiologische-farben]. As it fades, attend to it as to something hard to see [mod-newton-letter-sun-phantasm]. Well supported: that something is seen at once. Thin: independence from the flame. Never use the sun.

**Closed-eye darkness.** Daily, in a dark place, calm and unfed, eyes closed and unpressed, look into the middle of the dark [snt-sawan-singh-spiritual-gems, mod-mueller-phantastische-gesichtserscheinungen, hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]. Count any haze as the beginning [mod-sagan-awakening-third-eye, suf-kubra-fawaih-al-jamal]. If nothing comes, try Galton's deciphering attention [mod-galton-inquiries-human-faculty], or watch at the edge of sleep [mod-mueller-phantastische-gesichtserscheinungen]. Well supported: that a non-seer can find faint patterns. Unresolved: strain versus slackness.

**Lowered-lid gaze.** Lower the lids until the nose tip is just visible, rest attention between the brows, listen to the breath until it is inaudible, and do not care whether light comes, for a hundred days [dao-jinhua-zongzhi-chinese]. Classical prescription only.

**Hands over the eyes.** Close the eyes with the hands without pressing on the eyeballs, attend at the brows, and watch for a point; let it dissolve [hin-vijnana-bhairava-tantra, hin-avalon-serpent-power]. Two classical texts and Bates's patients agree that covered eyes yield light readily [mod-bates-perfect-sight]; omit the breath retention and pressure. Thin: no practitioner says this light outlasts the covering.

## 8. Gaps and confidence

Tibetan practice rests on three records, one pass_flagged, and no dark-retreat text has been read. Pressure-free continuation after eye pressure is claimed classically and contradicted by the only practitioner report. The Vijnanabhairava's commentaries, which might say how the point appears, are unread. The yantra-and-after-image search has been answered by the Bihar School's Sri Yantra trataka [hin-niranjanananda-dharana-darshan]; no free text yet links japa to seen light; Sivananda's Japa Yoga was read only in its introduction and is low confidence [hin-sivananda-japa-yoga]. Jewish practice has one procedure, which yields no light. The Sufi regime has no modern first-person witness. The Hesychast refusal and the open-eyed Christian testimony stand unreconciled. Galton remains the only non-seer who succeeded, and he found faint patterns, not steady light.
