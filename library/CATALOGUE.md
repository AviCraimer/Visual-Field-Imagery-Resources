### Upakkilesa Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 128): Corruptions

Pali Canon (Majjhima Nikaya), canonical; translation c. 2018. `bud-mn128-upakkilesa-sutta` Tier A.

- Mode: perceptual. Eyes: unspecified. Onset pathway: **development_only**. Relevance 5/5, practicality 3/5.
- Techniques: attention_to_spontaneous_phenomena, concentration_nonvisual
- Date: 2018 (published_record)
- Onset: Nothing for someone who sees nothing: light and vision of forms are already occurring in diligent, keen, resolute meditation and the sutra is troubleshooting why they vanish. The Buddha says the same happened to him before awakening. Time scale for stable light: a whole night, a whole day, even a whole night and day.

When light or visions of forms appear and then vanish, the Buddha says look for what knocked immersion out: doubt, loss of focus, dullness, terror, elation, discomfort, too much or too little energy, longing, perception of diversity, or too much concentration on forms. Notice each as it arises and give it up, one after another. Energy is like holding a quail: too tight kills it, too loose lets it fly. Once the corruptions are cleared, light may be seen without forms or forms without light, depending on whether attention rests on the basis of the light or of the forms. The breadth of the light follows the breadth of your immersion, and it can last a night and a day.

- *testimony* at Segment 15, source lines 139-143 (anchor verified): Anuruddha reports perceiving both light and vision of forms in diligent meditation, which then vanish.
- *caution* at Segment 16, source lines 144-152 (anchor verified): The refrain behind all eleven diagnoses: loss of immersion makes the light vanish.
- *method* at Segment 28, source lines 218-226 (anchor verified): Attending to the basis of the light gives light without forms, and attending to the basis of forms gives forms without light.
- *stages* at Segment 29, source lines 227-237 (anchor verified): Limited immersion gives limited light and forms; limitless immersion gives limitless light and forms.

### Anapanasati (Mindfulness of Breathing)

Buddhadasa Bhikkhu, Thai lectures 1959; English 1976. `bud-buddhadasa-anapanasati` Tier B.

- Mode: imaginal_crossing. Eyes: closed. Onset pathway: **induction**. Relevance 4/5, practicality 4/5.
- Techniques: concentration_nonvisual, visualization_crossing, breath_control, concentration_visual_object
- Date: 1976 (published_record)
- Onset: A procedure for producing the sign: count, connect, then narrow attention to the single point where air touches the nostril, visualise that point 'as a sensitive wound against which some object is rubbing', ignore the breath as a whole; this 'results in the formation of a mental image', the acquired sign (a white point or spot at the nose tip). If the counterpart sign does not appear it is because the breath is not felt at the contact point: breathe strongly and start again. Some objects (concepts such as Buddha's virtues) cannot yield an acquired sign at all. No time scale.

Begin by counting the breaths, then follow the whole breath without counting. When the mind is steady, drop the breath as a whole and put attention only on the point where the air touches the nostril, picturing it as a sensitive wound being rubbed as air passes. Attend to that point exclusively; a mental image forms there, typically a white point or spot (acquired sign). As the breath grows finer it changes; let it settle into a fixed form (counterpart sign): cotton, smoke, a pearl or gem at the nostril, a cobweb, lotus or wheel, or a large sun or moon. Then attend to the sign directly and let the breath run by itself. If the sign drifts to the chest, navel or in front of you, move your attention with it. Learn to enlarge, shrink and shift it, then fix it in one form until absorption.

### Discourses on the perception of light (aloka-sanna): AN 4.41, AN 7.61, SN 51.20, SN 51.11, AN 6.29, AN 8.64, AN 1.51-60

Pali Canon (Anguttara and Samyutta Nikayas), canonical; translation c. 2018. `bud-suttas-perception-of-light` Tier B.

- Mode: ambiguous. Eyes: unspecified. Onset pathway: **both**. Relevance 4/5, practicality 2/5.
- Techniques: other, concentration_nonvisual
- Date: 2018 (published_record)
- Onset: Induction only in the barest sense. The instruction is given to people who are not said to see anything (in AN 7.61 to a man nodding off at night): apply the mind to the perception of light, fix on the perception of day, by night exactly as by day, with a heart 'open and unenveloped'. No procedure for getting that perception is given. SN 51.20's own gloss is circular: the radiant mind is developed when one has 'properly grasped' the perception of light and of day. In AN 7.61 the step immediately before it is to get up, rinse the eyes, look round in every direction and look up at the stars; the text presents these as separate successive remedies and does not say one feeds the other. Development: AN 8.64 starts with light already perceived and adds forms 'after some time, living diligent, keen, and resolute'; no time scale beyond that phrase. Nothing on grace or on light being unforceable. Prerequisite in SN 51 is samadhi built on enthusiasm, energy, mind-development or inquiry, each neither lax nor tense, neither constricted by drowsiness nor scattered among the senses.

From these discourses alone the practice is one sentence long. Turn the mind to the perception of light and hold the perception of daytime, at night just as in the day and in the day just as at night, with the same features and signs at both times, keeping the heart open and unenveloped; this develops a mind full of radiance. Keep effort neither slack nor strained, the mind neither sunk in drowsiness nor scattered among the senses. If you are nodding off at night, try it after the simpler remedies have failed: changing the object, reflecting on and reciting the teaching, rubbing ears and limbs, standing up, rinsing the eyes and looking at the stars. Once light is perceived, the Buddha's own account is that he resolved to see forms as well, and after some time of diligent, keen, resolute practice did so, then extended his knowledge of what he saw round by round. How the perception of light is first obtained is not explained.

- *method* at AN 4.41 seg. 3.1-3.5, source lines 17-21 (anchor verified): The whole instruction: attend to the perception of light, fix on the perception of day, as by day so by night; with an open, unenveloped heart develop a radiant mind. ### Fawa'ih al-jamal wa-fawatih al-jalal (The Fragrances of Beauty and the Openings of Majesty)

Najm al-Din Kubra (d. 618/1221), c. 1200-1220; editions 1957 (Meier) and 1993 (Zaydan). `suf-kubra-fawaih-al-jamal` Tier A.

- Date: 1957 (published_record)

- Onset: The text speaks directly to the person who sees nothing (Meier sec. 2-6). If you close your eyes and say 'I see nothing', that is a mistake: you do see, but the 'darkness of your own existence' [ظلام الوجود] is too close to your inner sight to be noticed. To find it and see it in front of you with closed eyes, 'reduce something of your existence, or put some of it at a distance'. The means is striving [مجاهدة], fixed in three ways: (1) reduce food gradually; (2) give up your own choice to a trustworthy shaykh; (3) the way of Junayd, eight standing conditions: constant ablution, constant fasting, constant silence, constant seclusion [خلوة], constant dhikr of la ilaha illa'llah, constant binding of the heart to the shaykh and taking the knowledge of visionary events [علم الواقعات] from him, constant rejection of stray thoughts, constant non-objection to God (the tail of this clause is broken in the OCR; it ends 'or seeking refuge from a fire', which the editor's note takes as not asking for paradise or refuge from hell). Prerequisites: a shaykh; sound intention (his own first retreat failed for wrong motive and he was put out on the eleventh day); sitting, wakefulness and dhikr kept up. Time scales: forty days is the assumed retreat length but the cell is to be entered as one's grave with no end date; his head pains resolved in under a week; tongue-dhikr gives way to heart-dhikr 'after a long period'. On forcing: the power of dhikr shows 'in proportion to one's service of dhikr and perseverance in it', so effort counts; but it is hidden while 'existence' is strong and shows when existence weakens 'through sleep or trance', and success in his own case he ascribes to God's help for his sincerity.

Close your eyes and look. If there is nothing, that nothing is the darkness of your own existence, too close to be noticed; the work is to thin it. Under a shaykh, enter a cell as if it were your grave and keep eight things without break: ablution, fasting, silence, seclusion, the dhikr la ilaha illa'llah, the heart tied to the shaykh, stray thoughts refused, no quarrel with God. Cut food gradually; sit, stay awake, repeat. Expect darkness, then a black cloud, then fire, then green; sounds and pain in the head as dhikr settles there; later a spring of light in front, coloured fields at the edge of sight, circles at the eyes and brow, suns and stars. Tell the shaykh what you see. Judge each appearance by what you feel with it: clear, fast, rising fire with ease is dhikr; smoky, slow fire with a tight chest is not. Steady green is a settled heart. First seen in trance, then with closed eyes, the lights end up visible by day with eyes open.

### The Cure of Imperfect Sight by Treatment Without Glasses (Perfect Sight Without Glasses)

William H. Bates, 1920. `mod-bates-perfect-sight` Tier C. *Marginal.*

- Mode: perceptual. Eyes: both. Onset pathway: **both**. Relevance 3/5, practicality 3/5.
- Techniques: eye_pressure_or_sense_closure, darkness_exposure, attention_to_spontaneous_phenomena, concentration_visual_object, visualization_crossing, afterimage_retention, light_source_gazing
- Date: 1920 (published_record)
- Safety flags: sun_gazing
- Onset: Everything here must be read in reverse, since Bates's goal is a perfect black and the lights are his failure condition. Read that way it is an induction report: simply closing the eyes and covering them with the palms, without pressure, produces lights and colours at once in most of his patients, and a perfect black is rare ('some never succeed in doing it until they are cured'). Stated intensifiers: poor eyesight (the worse the sight the more numerous, vivid and persistent), fatigue, hunger, anger, worry, depression, illness, pain, and above all effort, namely trying to concentrate on black and to ignore the colours, which made them 'worse and worse'. Stated extinguishers: relaxation, a remembered small black object held 'one part best', rapid mental shifting, deliberately recalling many colours in turn. He says nothing to the person who sees only black except that this is the resting state. No prerequisites; no grace or transmission language. Time scale: immediate. Ch. XVI adds that illusions of colour, multiple lights and after-images can be produced at will by straining (staring, trying to see a whole letter equally well, looking above a light and trying to see it as well as when directly regarded).

Bates's procedure, and what it shows in reverse. Close the eyes and cover them with the cupped palms, fingers crossed on the forehead, with no pressure on the eyeballs and no light admitted. Most people do not then see black: they see grey streaks and clouds, flashes, patches of red, blue, green and yellow, up to kaleidoscopic displays that look fully seen. These are stronger with fatigue, hunger, worry, anger, illness or pain, and grow steadily worse when one tries hard to ignore them or to concentrate on blackness. Bates wants them gone: look at a black object, close the eyes, remember it easily with one small part blackest, shift lightly between black objects, and the field darkens; or cycle quickly through vivid remembered colours and then a small white patch, after which the ground turns black. Never force. For a practitioner seeking light, the report is that covered eyes yield lights readily, that strain, depletion and suppression feed them, and that relaxed attention to a small dark point quenches them. His sun-gazing advice should not be followed.

- *onset* at Ch. XII 'Palming', printed pp. 123-124, source lines 5056-5097 (anchor verified): Even with eyes closed and covered so that all light is excluded, patients do not see black but lights and colours, from an imperfect black to kaleidoscopic appearances that seem actually seen with the eyes. The worse the sight, the more numerous, vivid and persistent. Fatigue, hunger, anger, worry or depression make black harder to see. Reported forms: streaks or floating clouds of grey, flashes of light, patches of red, blue, green, yellow, moving clouds of black, black replaced after a few seconds by another colour.
- *mode* at Ch. XII 'Palming', printed pp. 125-126, source lines 5102-5124 (anchor verified): Some patients were so struck by the vividness of the colours that no argument could convince them they were not seeing them with their eyes. They granted that other people's closed-eye lights would be illusions but held their own to be reality, until they 'demonstrated the truth' for themselves.
- *method* at Ch. XII 'Palming', printed p. 126, source lines 5126-5141 (anchor verified): The antidote: look at a black object where its colour is seen best, close the eyes and remember the colour, repeat until memory seems equal to sight, then palm while holding the memory. If the memory is perfect the whole field goes black; if not within a few seconds, open the eyes and look again. Note that this is a remembered colour taking over the seen field.
- *caution* at Ch. XII 'Palming', printed p. 130, source lines 5282-5301 (anchor verified): Effort and concentration on the black cannot succeed. A patient who tried to see only black and ignore the kaleidoscopic colours found them growing worse the more she ignored them, went into convulsions from the strain, and needed a month's medical attendance. The fix was to stop palming and, eyes open, recall as many colours as possible, making the mind wander on purpose; after that she could palm for short periods.
- *caution* at Ch. XVII 'Vision Under Adverse Conditions', printed pp. 186-188, source lines 7389-7460 (anchor verified): After sun-gazing by people with defective sight: blind areas, the sun seen slate, yellow, red, blue or black, and patches of colour that persist from seconds to months, in one case a year or more after a few seconds' look; also hours of total blindness and inflammation. Bates calls all of it temporary and a mental illusion, and still recommends sun-gazing with care, even focusing the sun on the eye with a burning glass. Dangerous advice.
- *interpretation* at Ch. XVI 'Illusions of Imperfect and of Normal Eyesight', printed pp. 178-180, source lines 7112-7189 (anchor verified): Bates rejects retinal fatigue as the cause of after-images: closed eyes cannot see, so anything seen then is a mental illusion showing loss of mental control. He adds that anyone can produce an after-image at will by straining (trying to see the big C 'all alike'), and multiple lights by looking above a light and trying to see it as well as when looked at directly.
